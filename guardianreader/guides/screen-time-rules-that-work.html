Family Rules

Screen time rules that actually work

Most families have had a screen time rule. Fewer have one that is still running a month later. The rule is rarely wrong in principle; it fails because it was built in a way that required something no household can supply every single day.

A guide for parents · Updated 2026-08-11

Understanding the failure mode is more useful than another list of recommended hours.

Why rules collapse

  • They need an adult present. Any rule enforced by watching dies the first evening you are cooking, working or out.
  • They are negotiable. One successful appeal teaches your child that the rule is an opening offer, and every evening becomes a fresh negotiation.
  • They punish without offering an alternative. Taking the phone away creates a bored child and no plan, which is the state in which workarounds get invented.
  • They are too ambitious. A rule that fails on ordinary Tuesdays is not a rule, it is an aspiration, and children read the difference immediately.

The three properties of rules that survive

  1. Automatic. It should run whether or not you are in the room, awake, or in the mood for a confrontation.
  2. Constant. The same rule tomorrow, and next Tuesday. Stability is what makes it stop feeling personal.
  3. Earnable. There has to be a way for your child to get what they want by doing something reasonable. A rule with no path through it is just a wall to climb.

Sample rules by age

These are starting points, not prescriptions. Adjust after a week of watching what actually happens:

  • 4 to 6: one page read out loud unlocks 15 minutes. No screens in the hour before bed.
  • 7 to 9: two pages unlock 30 minutes. Devices charge outside the bedroom overnight.
  • 10 to 12: three pages unlock 30 to 45 minutes. Homework first on school nights.
  • 13+: four pages unlock 45 minutes, negotiated with them rather than imposed on them. At this age, buy-in is worth more than control.

Enforcing without policing

The goal is to remove yourself from the enforcement loop entirely. Not because supervision is bad, but because your credibility is a limited resource and spending it on a nightly timer dispute is a poor use of it.

When the rule is enforced by the device, disagreements stop being about you. You get to be the person who reads with them, rather than the person who takes the phone.

A rule the phone enforces

Guardian Reader is built to satisfy all three properties. It runs automatically through Apple's Screen Time system, applies the same rule every day, and always leaves a path through it: read a page out loud, earn the minutes.

When the time runs out the apps lock again on their own, and the settings stay behind a parent PIN so the rule cannot be edited by the person it applies to.

The best fonts for subtitles

Updated 2026-08-07 · Charlatan for iPhone

A subtitle has a harder job than almost any other type on screen. It sits on top of moving footage, it has to survive a bright background it cannot control, it is read on a phone held at arm's length, and it is gone in under two seconds.

That job decides the answer. The best subtitle font is not the prettiest one: it is the one that stays legible under all of those conditions at once.

Weight matters more than the typeface

If you change one thing, change the weight. A bold or black cut of an ordinary sans will outperform a beautiful typeface set in regular, every time, because thin strokes disappear against busy footage.

This is also why so many creators land on the same handful of faces without coordinating: Impact, Bebas Neue, Montserrat Bold, and heavy grotesques generally. They are not fashionable choices, they are survivable ones.

An outline is not decoration

White text on a white wall is invisible, and you do not always control what is behind you. A black stroke around the letters, or a solid marker-style highlight behind them, is what makes a caption independent of the footage.

This is the single most common mistake in homemade captions: perfect font, no outline, unreadable the moment the subject walks past a window.

Condensed buys you room, caps cost you speed

A condensed face fits more words on one line without shrinking them, which is why it is the default for anything with long words: Spanish and German especially.

ALL CAPS looks confident and is genuinely good for short bursts of two or three words. For full sentences it is slower to read, because we recognise lowercase words partly by their shape and caps flattens every word into the same rectangle. Use caps for punches, sentence case for explanations.

What to avoid

  • Light and thin weights of anything: they vanish over footage.
  • High-contrast serifs like Didot or Bodoni, where the thin strokes break up at small sizes. Beautiful on a poster, gone on a phone.
  • Script and handwriting faces for anything longer than three words.
  • Pure white with no outline or shadow, unless your background is genuinely dark and stays that way.
  • More than one font in the same video. Pick one and let it become the thing people recognise.

How to do it in Charlatan

  1. Open your video in Charlatan and tap Fonts. The 99 typefaces are grouped by the job they do: Impact, Sans, Condensed, and so on.
  2. Tap through them with the video visible. Each one renders on your own footage, which is the only test that matters.
  3. Switch to Style and turn on the outline, or the marker highlight if your background is busy.
  4. Try ALL CAPS with the Punch pace, and sentence case with the Phrase pace, to feel the difference the section above describes.
  5. Save the combination as a style. It will be one tap on every video after this, which is how a channel starts looking like one channel.

Questions people also ask

What font does Netflix use for subtitles?

Netflix specifies a small set of clean sans faces, and the point is not the name: it is that they are medium-to-bold weights with a drop shadow, sized generously. The principle transfers: heavy enough to survive the image, with something behind it for contrast.

Should subtitles be in all caps?

For two or three word bursts, yes: it reads fast and looks deliberate. For full sentences, no: lowercase is measurably quicker to read because word shapes help recognition, and caps removes them.
